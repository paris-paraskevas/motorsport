Formula 1 has torn up the rule book for 2026 — by the sport's own account, the biggest set of regulation changes in its history, all landing in a single season. New power units, new aerodynamics, smaller cars, sustainable fuel and a reshaped grid. Here's what actually changed, in plain English.

## Power units: a 50/50 hybrid

The headline is under the engine cover. F1 keeps its 1.6-litre V6 turbo hybrid, but the balance of where the power comes from has shifted dramatically: roughly **50% from the internal combustion engine and 50% from the electrical side**, up from something closer to an 80/20 split before.

To get there, the cars lose the complex MGU-H heat-recovery system entirely, while the MGU-K — which harvests and deploys energy under braking and acceleration — gets far more powerful, with its maximum output rising from 120 kW to **up to 350 kW**. Despite the rebalance, the cars still produce **more than 1,000 bhp**; the power just arrives from different places, with far more of it electrical.

## Active aero — and the end of DRS

For the first time, F1 cars have **full-time active aerodynamics**: both the front and rear wings move depending on where the car is on the track. There are two modes — a low-drag **"Straight" mode** that flattens the wings for top speed, and a higher-downforce **"Corner" mode** for grip through the bends. The front wing moves too, to keep the car balanced as the rear changes.

Because every car can now shed drag on the straights, the old **Drag Reduction System (DRS) is gone**. In its place is **"Overtake mode"**: get within one second of the car ahead at a set point on the track, and for the next lap you get access to extra electrical deployment to help you attack. The crucial difference from DRS — which only the chasing car could use, on set straights — is that the new boost is available to **every car, anywhere on the lap**, provided they've harvested the energy for it.

## Smaller, lighter, nimbler cars

The 2026 cars are physically smaller. The wheelbase shrinks from 360 cm to 340 cm, the width from 200 cm to 190 cm, and the minimum weight drops by around 30 kg. The tyres are narrower too. The goal is more agile cars that race better — especially at tight venues like Monaco and Singapore.

The FIA also retuned the aerodynamics so cars can follow each other more closely: the target is for a car running 20 metres behind a rival to keep about **90% of its downforce** (versus a number that had slipped toward 70% under the previous rules). The trade-off is overall pace — the 2026 cars are expected to be roughly **two seconds a lap slower** than the 2022–25 generation, though that gap typically shrinks as teams develop.

## Sustainable fuel

For the first time, the power units run on **Advanced Sustainable Fuel** — made from sources like carbon capture, municipal waste and non-food biomass, and independently certified. It was trialled in F2 and F3 in 2025 before arriving in F1.

## A reshaped grid

The new rules — and especially the simpler, more road-relevant power unit — pulled new manufacturers in:

- **Audi** enters as a full works team (having taken over Sauber) with its own power unit.
- **Cadillac** joins as an all-new **eleventh team** — the first time the grid has expanded past ten since 2016 — running Ferrari power to start.
- **Honda** is now tied to **Aston Martin** as a works engine partner.
- **Ford** is back, supporting **Red Bull Powertrains**.
- **Alpine** switches to **Mercedes** power.

## Will it make for better racing?

That's the bet. The smaller cars and the closer-following aero are meant to produce more wheel-to-wheel racing, while "Overtake mode" tries to keep passing possible without the artificial feel DRS was often accused of.

Not everyone's convinced. Lewis Hamilton called the package "ridiculously complex" during pre-season testing, and Max Verstappen has been blunt about the electrical-heavy power units. The truth will only show on track over the season — which is exactly what makes 2026 worth watching.
