**Rally, or rally racing, is a form of motorsport in which cars are driven one at a time against the clock rather than wheel-to-wheel.** A rally is not a single race around a circuit but a series of timed runs, called special stages, held on closed roads whose surface can be anything from gravel and tarmac to snow and ice. Each car carries two people, a driver and a co-driver, and the crew with the lowest combined stage time at the end of the event, after any penalties are added, wins. The premier series is the [World Rally Championship (WRC)](/series/wrc).

## What a rally actually is

Most motorsport is a straight fight: cars line up together, race wheel-to-wheel, and the first across the line wins. Rally works differently. Cars start **one at a time**, at set intervals (often a couple of minutes apart), and each crew races **against the clock** rather than against the car in front. There is no jostling for position the way circuit racing has it, because rivals are rarely on the same piece of road together.

A rally is run in a **point-to-point** format over a route made up of many separate sections. The parts that count are the **special stages**: sections of road closed to the public (sometimes minor public roads, sometimes private or forest tracks) and timed to the tenth of a second, ranging from a couple of kilometres to around 30 kilometres each. A crew's times from every stage are added together across the whole event, and the lowest total wins. Everything else, the car, the strategy, the navigation, exists to shave time off those stages.

## The crew: driver and co-driver

Every rally car carries **two people**. The **driver** controls the car; the **co-driver** sits alongside reading out **pace notes**, a shorthand description of the road ahead. Because a stage is driven flat out, often only once and frequently over blind crests and unfamiliar roads, the driver cannot memorise it the way a circuit racer learns a lap. The co-driver's calls, delivered a few seconds before each feature arrives, tell the driver which way the next corner goes, how sharp it is, how far away it is, and what hazards to expect (a crest, a jump, adverse camber, or a firm "don't cut" where a rock or ditch lurks on the inside).

Pace notes are written during **reconnaissance**, known as the "recce", when the crew drives through each stage beforehand at strictly limited speed to record every corner and hazard. Corners are usually graded on a numbered scale of severity, though there is no single universal system: each crew agrees its own notation and sticks to it. The partnership runs on total trust, because the driver commits to corners they often cannot yet see, acting on the co-driver's word alone.

## Special stages, road sections and service parks

A rally is a repeating loop of three ingredients:

- **Special stages** are the timed, competitive sections on closed roads. They are the only part where speed sets the result.
- **Road sections** (also called liaison or transit sections) are the ordinary public roads that link the stages together. Crews drive their own rally cars along them in normal traffic, obeying every speed limit and traffic law, to reach the next stage start by a set target time. Speed on a road section never improves your result, and arriving late (or early) at a control draws a time penalty.
- The **service park** is the central base where mechanics repair and prepare the cars, but only inside strict time windows (a brief splash of a few minutes between stages, or a longer session at the end of the day). Overrun the allotted time and the crew takes a penalty; away from the service park, usually only the driver and co-driver may work on the car.

Most events also open with a **shakedown**, a short test run that lets crews check their set-up before the timing that counts begins.

## The surfaces: gravel, tarmac, snow and ice

One of rally's defining features is that it is not confined to a smooth racetrack. Stages are run on whatever roads a region offers, and the surface changes completely from event to event:

- **Gravel** is the most common rally surface, loose and constantly shifting as each car sweeps a line through it.
- **Tarmac** (asphalt) rewards a smoother, more circuit-like style and calls for different tyres and set-up.
- **Snow and ice** define winter rounds such as the season-opening Monte-Carlo Rally, which mixes dry tarmac with black ice over Alpine mountain passes, and Rally Sweden, run on packed snow where studded tyres bite into banks of ice.

Individual rounds have their own character. Rally Finland is the fastest in the championship, run on smooth, flowing gravel punctuated by huge jumps, while Safari Rally Kenya is the roughest, a punishing mix of rock, sand and water crossings where simply reaching the finish is an achievement.

## How the winner is decided

A rally is scored on **cumulative time**. Each crew's times from every special stage are added up, and the crew with the **lowest total** across the whole event wins. Within a single rally there are no points for finishing position; a few tenths saved on each of twenty-odd stages can decide the outcome.

**Penalties** are added straight onto that total. Arriving late at a time control or overrunning the allowed service time adds time to a crew's total (commonly counted in seconds for every minute of lateness), and serious lateness or missing a stage brings far larger penalties or even exclusion. This is why rally rewards not just outright speed but consistency, mechanical care and precise timekeeping.

At championship level, points are then awarded on top of the individual event results. The [WRC points system](/information/rally/how-wrc-points-work) gives championship points to the leading finishers, with bonus points for the fastest crews on the final day and on the closing "Power Stage".

## The WRC and other kinds of rallying

The **[World Rally Championship](/information/rally/what-is-the-wrc)** is the pinnacle of the sport. Run by the **FIA** and contested since **1973**, it visits roughly a dozen countries a season and awards separate titles to drivers, co-drivers and manufacturers. Below it sits a clear ladder: the **European Rally Championship (ERC)**, the oldest international rally championship, and a network of **national championships** where most crews begin. For the shape of a top-level event, see [how a WRC rally weekend works](/information/rally/how-a-wrc-rally-weekend-works).

"Rally" is also an umbrella term for several related disciplines that work quite differently:

- **Stage rallying**, described above, is what the WRC and most national series run.
- **Rally raid** (or cross-country rally), such as the **Dakar Rally**, uses the same against-the-clock idea but over enormous desert and off-road distances, and crews navigate from a **roadbook** issued shortly before the stage rather than from pre-made pace notes.
- **Rallycross** is the odd one out: short, wheel-to-wheel races with several cars starting together on a mixed tarmac-and-gravel circuit, closer to circuit racing than to stage rally.
- **Historic rallying** runs older cars, sometimes on a regularity format that rewards precise timing over outright speed.

## How rally differs from circuit racing

Rally and circuit racing are both motorsport, yet almost every fundamental differs:

- **You race the clock, not the pack.** Crews run one at a time and are timed; there is no wheel-to-wheel dicing for position.
- **The course is new every time.** Instead of learning one permanent circuit, crews face unfamiliar roads they see only briefly in recce, which is why pace notes and a co-driver exist.
- **There are two people in the car**, not one.
- **The surface and weather are part of the challenge**, shifting from gravel to tarmac to snow across a season rather than staying on smooth asphalt.
- **An event lasts days, not hours**, and is decided by cumulative time plus penalties rather than a single finishing order.

## Rally in short

- **What:** cars driven one at a time against the clock, not wheel-to-wheel.
- **Where:** timed special stages on closed roads, linked by ordinary public road sections.
- **Who:** a two-person crew, a driver plus a co-driver reading pace notes.
- **Surfaces:** gravel, tarmac, snow and ice, varying from event to event.
- **Winner:** the lowest cumulative stage time after penalties.
- **Top series:** the FIA World Rally Championship (WRC).
