**MotoGP**, **Moto2** and **Moto3** are the three classes of the Grand Prix motorcycle world championship, and they all race at the same events as a single ladder of speed and experience. The quickest way to tell them apart is by engine size and by how much freedom each class gives engineers:

- **Moto3** is the entry class, run on small **250cc single-cylinder** four-stroke bikes with tightly controlled costs.
- **Moto2** is the intermediate class, a spec formula where every rider uses the same **765cc three-cylinder Triumph engine**.
- **MotoGP** is the pinnacle, contested on roughly **1,000cc prototype** machines built by the manufacturers.

## The three-class ladder: Moto3 to Moto2 to MotoGP

All three classes share every race weekend, running back to back at the same circuit, so a Grand Prix fan sees the full ladder in one day. They are designed as a progression: Moto3 develops the youngest riders on the least powerful bikes, Moto2 tests them on a level playing field, and [MotoGP](/series/motogp) rewards the best of them with the fastest prototypes in the sport. The whole structure sits under Dorna's "Road to MotoGP" pathway, which also feeds in junior and rookie categories below Moto3. You can browse more explainers on the top class in the [MotoGP answers hub](/information/motogp).

## What cc is Moto3, and what makes it the entry class

Moto3 bikes use a **250cc single-cylinder four-stroke** engine. The formula is deliberately restrictive: engines are normally aspirated (no turbocharging or supercharging), the bore is capped at 81mm, and the rev limit is 14,000 rpm. A Moto3 machine has a minimum weight of about 148 kg including the rider and produces around 55 horsepower, which makes it the slowest of the three classes.

Because the bikes are so evenly matched and low on power, Moto3 racing is defined by slipstreaming and huge braking-zone battles, and it rewards racecraft over machinery. Costs and electronics are tightly controlled, and the bikes run control **Pirelli** tyres (the class switched from Dunlop to Pirelli in 2024). Current bikes are built by manufacturers such as **KTM** and **Honda**. Riders here are typically the youngest on the grid.

## What is Moto2, and is Moto2 a spec series?

Moto2 is effectively a **spec (one-engine) series** for its powertrain. Every bike on the grid uses the same **765cc three-cylinder** engine supplied by **Triumph**, based on the Street Triple 765 RS road bike. Triumph has been the exclusive Moto2 engine supplier since 2019, when it replaced the previous 600cc four-cylinder Honda unit, and its contract runs through 2029. The race-tuned triple produces more than 138 horsepower, and, as in Moto3, all teams run control **Pirelli** tyres.

What Moto2 does not standardise is the **chassis**. Teams buy their frames from independent constructors, and the grid is dominated by Germany's **Kalex**, which has taken the constructors' title every year from 2013 to 2025, with Italy's **Boscoscuro** as its main rival. So the honest answer to "is Moto2 a spec series?" is: the engine and tyres are identical for everyone, while the chassis and setup are the main variables. That is what makes Moto2 the final proving ground before the top class, where results reflect the rider and the team rather than engine power.

## What cc is MotoGP, and why it is the prototype class

MotoGP is the prototype pinnacle. The engine formula allows up to **1,000cc**, four-stroke, with a maximum of **four cylinders** and a maximum bore of 81mm. Manufacturers are free to choose their engine layout, so the grid mixes inline-four and V4 designs. These are bespoke racing machines, not production-based bikes, built by **Ducati, Aprilia, KTM, Honda and Yamaha**.

A current MotoGP bike makes more than 280 horsepower, has a minimum weight of 157 kg, and tops 350 km/h, with the fastest bikes now exceeding 360 km/h at high-speed circuits such as Mugello. The class runs control **Michelin** tyres. Even here, some hardware is standardised for fairness: since 2016 every bike uses a unified electronic control unit (a spec ECU built by Magneti Marelli) running common software. So MotoGP is a true prototype class in its engines and chassis, with only the electronics and tyres held in common.

## Which classes are spec, and which are prototype?

This is the core of the difference between Moto2 and MotoGP, and between Moto2 and Moto3:

- **Moto3** is a tightly controlled prototype class, not a single-make series. Manufacturers build the bikes to a very restrictive rulebook, with capped costs, a standard-type ECU and control tyres.
- **Moto2** is the most spec of the three: one control engine and control tyres for the whole field, with the chassis as the main area of open competition.
- **MotoGP** is the true prototype class: bespoke engines and chassis from each manufacturer, with only the ECU, its software and the tyres standardised.

In short, freedom for engineers increases as you climb the ladder, while the racing at the lower levels is engineered to be closer and cheaper.

## How riders move up the ladder

A rider's career normally follows the ladder from the bottom up: Moto3 first, then Moto2, then MotoGP. Standout junior riders are promoted class by class, and a strong Moto2 season is the usual gateway to a MotoGP seat. It is much like the way a car racer climbs the [feeder-series ladder](/information/feeder-series) toward Formula 1, with each rung asking for more speed, consistency and technical feedback than the last.

## What changes for 2027

The 2026 season is the last under the current formula, and MotoGP engines are frozen for the year ahead of a reset. From 2027, MotoGP engine capacity drops from 1,000cc to 850cc, the maximum bore falls from 81mm to 75mm, and the fuel tank shrinks from 22 to 20 litres. MotoGP will also switch its tyre supplier from Michelin to **Pirelli**, bringing the top class into line with Moto2 and Moto3, which already run Pirelli. There is a fuller rundown in [what's new in MotoGP for 2026](/information/motogp/whats-new-in-motogp-2026).

## MotoGP vs Moto2 vs Moto3 in short

- **Moto3:** 250cc single-cylinder four-stroke, tightly restricted entry class, around 55 hp, Pirelli tyres.
- **Moto2:** 765cc three-cylinder spec Triumph engine shared by all riders, open chassis (Kalex and Boscoscuro), more than 138 hp, Pirelli tyres.
- **MotoGP:** roughly 1,000cc four-stroke prototypes from five manufacturers, more than 280 hp, unified spec ECU, Michelin tyres, over 350 km/h.
- **The ladder:** riders climb Moto3, then Moto2, then MotoGP, in rising order of speed and freedom.
