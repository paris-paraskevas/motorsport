Start with the part nobody tells you: **no team publishes what it pays its drivers, and no regulation requires it to.** Every salary figure you have read for a current driver is a journalistic estimate. That does not make the history unknowable, because a handful of deals have entered the public record and the regulations themselves say a great deal, but it does mean the honest version of this answer separates what is documented from what is reported.

## The shape of the change, in three eras

**Start money and prize shares.** In the championship's early decades a driver's income came largely from the organisers and the results: appearance money for turning up, a share of the prize fund for finishing well, and often several cars in several categories on the same weekend to make a living. A manufacturer contract was a seat far more than it was a salary.

**The sponsorship era.** Once tobacco and consumer money arrived, the money moved from the organisers to the teams, and the teams began paying retainers. A driver's earnings became a negotiation with an employer rather than a function of the weekend, and personal endorsement deals became a second income running alongside the first.

**The modern retainer.** A leading driver is now paid a base retainer, plus performance bonuses, plus whatever their own name earns away from the team. The published numbers you see are attempts to add those parts up from outside.

## The documented marker: Senna, 1993

The clearest hard datum in the whole story is **Ayrton Senna's 1993 McLaren contract**, because the team has talked about it. Senna raced that season on a **race-by-race deal at $1 million per race**: five races first, then the remaining eleven of the sixteen-race season at the same rate, **$16 million for the year**. The arrangement carried a condition that the money had to be in his account by the Wednesday before each race, which made a season-long contract terminable week to week.

Martin Whitmarsh, then McLaren's operations director, put it plainly: *"we were paying Ayrton a million dollars a race"*, in a year the team lost £1.5 million.

That is the turn, stated in one number. A driver was suddenly worth more per weekend than most of the field earned in a season.

## The scale that followed: Schumacher

**Michael Schumacher** is the reference point for what the retainer era grew into. Forbes puts his **career earnings at around $1 billion**, ranks him **fifth among the highest-paid athletes of all time**, and counts him among only six athletes ever to have topped its annual highest-paid list. Those are aggregate career figures rather than a salary, which is exactly the kind of number that can be reported reliably from outside a contract.

## Why comparing Verstappen or Hamilton with Senna or Prost is harder than it looks

Four reasons the like-for-like comparison people want does not really exist:

1. **Inflation and currency.** A 1993 dollar is not a 2026 dollar, and several of the great deals of that era were struck in lire, francs or pounds.
2. **The parts are not the same.** A modern package is a retainer plus bonuses plus image rights. An older one might be a smaller retainer plus a real share of prize money.
3. **Endorsements have grown faster than salaries.** For the biggest names, what the helmet earns away from the team can rival what the team pays.
4. **Only the length is public.** For current deals, the durations get announced and the money does not. Max Verstappen's Red Bull extension, announced at Zandvoort in 2026, runs **to the end of 2030**; the figure attached to it is press estimate, not disclosure.

## The regulation that explains the ceiling: there isn't one

Here is the part with a genuine paper trail. Formula 1 has run a **cost cap** on teams since 2021, and for 2026 it is set at **US$215,000,000**, adjusted for the number of races and for indexation. It covers car design and development, race operations and most staff.

**Driver salaries are explicitly excluded from it.** So are the salaries of each team's three highest-paid staff, along with marketing, hospitality and several other categories. A team can therefore spend whatever it likes on its drivers without touching the budget it races on, which is precisely why the top of the market has kept climbing while team spending has been held flat.

That carve-out has been contested since the cap was written. F1 set up a **working group to look at capping driver and senior-management pay**, and the idea resurfaces regularly, usually as a soft cap that would trade excess driver spend against development budget. As of the 2026 season **no driver salary cap exists**. It is a discussion, not a regulation.

## How to read any salary number you see

A quick test that works on almost every list published: ask who could possibly know. If the figure is a career total assembled from public earnings, treat it as reasonable. If it is this year's base salary for a driver still under contract, treat it as an informed guess, however confidently it is printed.
