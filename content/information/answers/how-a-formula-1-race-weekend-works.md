A Formula 1 weekend is **three days long and comes in two shapes**. Most rounds use the standard format, where everything builds towards Sunday. Six rounds in 2026 use the Sprint format, which turns Friday and Saturday into competitive days of their own.

## The standard weekend

**Friday** is for learning the track. Two one-hour practice sessions, **FP1** and **FP2**, let teams try setups, run through tyre compounds and gather the long-run data that decides Sunday's strategy. Nothing here counts for points.

**Saturday** opens with a third hour of practice, **FP3**, and then the weekend turns serious with **qualifying**.

**Sunday** is the Grand Prix.

## Qualifying, and why it is three parts

Qualifying decides the starting grid, and it eliminates the slowest cars in stages.

- **Q1 — 18 minutes.** Everyone runs. The six slowest are out and fill the back of the grid.
- **Q2 — 15 minutes.** The remaining 16 run. Six more are out, setting the grid from 11th to 16th.
- **Q3 — 13 minutes.** The last ten fight for pole.

The six-per-segment figure is a **2026 detail worth knowing**: with Cadillac joining, the field grew to 22 cars, and the regulations scale the eliminations with the entry list. It was five per segment when 20 cars raced.

## The Grand Prix

A Grand Prix is not a fixed number of laps. It is the **fewest laps needed to exceed 305 kilometres**, which is why lap counts differ so much from circuit to circuit. **Monaco is the exception** at 260 kilometres, because it is slow enough that the full distance would take too long. A race generally runs to a **two-hour limit**.

## Sprint weekends

Six of 2026's rounds add a Sprint: **China, Miami, Canada, Great Britain, the Netherlands and Singapore**. The day-by-day shape changes completely.

**Friday** — one practice session, then **Sprint Qualifying**, a compressed version of the usual format at **12, 10 and 8 minutes** for SQ1, SQ2 and SQ3. It sets the grid for the Sprint, not for the Grand Prix.

**Saturday** — the **Sprint** itself, then a full **Grand Prix qualifying** session later the same day. The Sprint covers **100 kilometres**, around a third of a Grand Prix and roughly 30 minutes of racing, with **no mandatory pit stop**. Points go to the **top eight finishers, eight down to one**.

**Sunday** — the Grand Prix, exactly as on a normal weekend.

The trade is one practice session for two extra competitive ones. Teams arrive with far less data and have to commit to a setup almost immediately, which is the point.

## Parc fermé: why Saturday is the deadline

From the moment a car leaves the pit lane in qualifying until the race starts, it is in **parc fermé** and its setup is frozen. Teams can change tyres and do routine work, but the car that qualifies has to be the car that races. Break that and the driver starts from the pit lane.

A Sprint weekend runs **two separate parc fermé windows** — one from Sprint Qualifying to the end of the Sprint, then a genuine working gap before qualifying, after which the car is locked down again for Sunday.

## One car, twice a season, goes to a rookie

Every full-time driver must **stand aside for a rookie in two practice sessions a year**, which works out at four rookie FP1 outings per team. It is why an unfamiliar name sometimes appears in Friday morning timing screens.
